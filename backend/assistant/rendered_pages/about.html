About Tesserae

What is Tesserae?

Tesserae is a free, open tool for finding connections between texts. Its central tool is the search for intertexts, the places where one author echoes, adapts or quotes another. Give it two works and it finds the passages that share words, phrases, rare vocabulary, sound, syntax or meaning, in one language or across languages. The Reader shows a text with its parallels, similar passages, later quotations, commentary and translation at the side. Theme Search finds the passages about a scene or an idea you describe in English, in one text, in two texts compared, or across the whole corpus at once. Inscriptions and papyri, coins, museum objects and dated events sit beside the texts, so a passage can be read next to the documents and things of its world.

The corpus holds more than 5,300 texts in Latin, Greek, English, Hebrew, Coptic, Persian and Urdu, from Homer to the Renaissance and beyond, with an English translation wherever a public-domain one exists. Founded in 2008 by Neil Coffee (Department of Classics) and J. P. Koenig (Department of Linguistics) at the University at Buffalo, SUNY, Tesserae is now in its sixth version, rebuilt from the ground up around the scoring its earlier versions were known for.

The name "Tesserae" refers to the small tiles used in Roman mosaics. Just as these tiles combine to form larger images, textual parallels reveal the interconnected nature of classical literature.

Key Features

Intertext Search

Compare two works, or one work against the whole corpus. Lemma, exact form, sound, edit distance, semantic embeddings, dictionary synonyms, syntax, rare words and quotation are weighed together, and every result shows what it rests on.

Across Languages

A passage finds its counterpart in another language through dictionaries and a model of meaning, between Greek and Latin, Latin and English, Hebrew and Greek, and Coptic and Greek.

Theme Search

Describe a scene or an idea in English and get the passages about it, from every language at once, ranked by meaning and marked with how confident the match is.

The Reader

Read a text with a gutter that marks every line’s connections. Open its parallels, similar passages, quotations in later writers, commentary notes, articles and a translation beside it.

Theme Search, one text

Ask one work what it says about a subject. The passages come back in order of fit, on a strip that shows where in the work they fall, with a short account of what the work says and what the commentaries say.

History

Inscriptions and papyri, coins, museum objects and dated events, searchable beside the literature they quote or echo, each linked to its source collection.

Technical Details

  • Lemmatization via lookup tables built from Universal Dependencies treebanks, with CLTK (Classical Language Toolkit) as the fallback for Latin and Greek; the ETCBC/BHSA morphology for Hebrew, with Stanza as the fallback; Stanza for Persian and Urdu
  • NLTK for English text processing
  • A passage index of about 1,000,000 windows, each with a model-written description and an embedding, behind Theme Search, Similar Passages and the Reader
  • SPhilBERTa model for cross-lingual semantic similarity
  • Universal Dependencies treebank support for syntax analysis
  • Metrical scansion data integrated from MQDQ/Pede Certo

What the site records

To understand how the site is used, it records which pages a browser opens during one visit, the network address and the city and country that address suggests, and the searches run. A visit is one browser tab's session, identified by a random token that is discarded when the tab closes. No account or personal detail is involved, and nothing is sent to an outside analytics service.

Text Sources

The texts used in this project were gathered from many electronic text databases. Latin and Greek texts come from The Latin Library, The Perseus Project, DigilibLT, Open Greek and Latin Project, Musisque Deoque, and Corpus Scriptorum Latinorum. The Greek New Testament is The Greek New Testament: SBL Edition (SBLGNT), edited by Michael W. Holmes, ©2010 Society of Biblical Literature and Logos Bible Software, licensed under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0. Coptic texts (Sahidic and Bohairic) come from Coptic Scriptorium (CC-BY 4.0; the Sahidica New Testament is additionally subject to its own academic-use license, ©2000–2006 J. Warren Wells). The Hebrew Bible text is the Miqra according to the Masorah (MAM) edition, based on the Aleppo Codex, obtained through Sefaria (CC-BY-SA); Hebrew morphological lemmatization draws on the ETCBC/BHSA dataset (Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia Amstelodamensis; CC-BY-NC 4.0, DOI 10.17026/dans-z6y-skyh) via Text-Fabric. The Hebrew semantic channel uses MiqraBERT (D. M. Smiley), fine-tuned in-house on OpenBible.info cross-references (CC-BY, from the Treasury of Scripture Knowledge); Hebrew-to-Greek Septuagint matching uses the CATSS Masoretic-Septuagint parallel (E. Tov), bridged through Greek to the Latin Vulgate for Hebrew-to-Latin matching. We have modified the texts by changing the markup, and may have made superficial changes to orthography. During our searches, all punctuation and capitalization are removed.
